We have created a series of AFCA Approach documents, such as this one, to help consumers and
financial firms better understand how we reach decisions about key issues.

These documents explain the way we approach some common issues and complaint types that we see at
AFCA. However, it is important to understand that each complaint that comes to us is unique, so this
information is a guide only. No determination (decision) can be seen as a precedent for future cases, and
no AFCA Approach document can cover everything you might want to know about key issues.

### 1 Purpose of this Approach

#### 1.1 About AFCA’s Approach documents

AFCA Approach documents support consumers, small businesses and financial firms’
understanding of AFCA’s approach to certain issues and how we reach our decisions. 
The Approach documents provide practical information on how AFCA will assess and 
determine complaints and enhance AFCA’s transparency, consistency, and efficiency.

#### 1.2 Scope

This document sets out how AFCA approaches complaints relating to general 
insurance claims. It forms part of a broader suite of Approaches on how AFCA 
resolves complaints on specific topics.

This document does not relate to complaints about life insurance claims.

#### 1.3 Summary

Insurers have key obligations under the *Insurance Contracts Act 1984 (Cth)* (the Act),
including the duty of utmost good faith, which requires them to handle insurance 
claims fairly, transparently, and efficiently. Insurance claims handling and settling 
services are also regulated as a financial service under the Corporations Act, and the 
obligations in sections 912A and 912B apply to them.

This document provides information about how AFCA considers various types of 
complaints relating to the handling of general insurance claims considering an 
insurer’s legal obligations and applicable industry standards.

### 2 Claims handling

#### 2.1 Resolving claims fairly

#### Insurers obligations

Insurers have an obligation to handle and resolve an insurance claim consistently with 
the duty of utmost good faith and in accordance with obligations in relevant legislation 
(e.g. section 912A of the *Corporations Act 2001).* Utmost good faith is an implied term 
of all insurance contracts and applies to both parties. It is not limited to the formation 
of the contract but extends throughout its duration. It includes a range of concepts, 
such as:

• acting with honesty and propriety

• not acting capriciously or unreasonably

• acting in accordance with commercial standards of decency and fairness and with 
due regard to the insured’s interests.

In the context of claims handling, this would include:

• making timely responses to a claim for indemnity

• handling an insurance claim fairly and reasonably promptly.

There is no standard formula as to what constitutes a reasonable time or how exactly 
a claim is handled fairly. This will depend on the specific circumstances of the claim 
and the conduct of the parties.

The General Insurance Code of Practice (GICOP) includes good guidance about 
timeframes and conduct. AFCA will have regard to the GICOP version that applies to 
the complaint. However, these are standard timeframes. Meeting those timeframes is 
not, of itself, conclusive about whether a claim has been handled fairly or reasonably 
promptly.

For example, under the GICOP, insurers must make a decision on a claim within 4 
months (paragraph 77).<sup>1</sup> However, a simple claim for a broken window that is plainly 
covered should not take this long to accept. Therefore, in complaints involving claims 
handling, AFCA will consider whether the insurer has met its general claims handling 
obligations.

An insurer’s obligations usually extend to contractors or agents they use to help them 
manage the claim.

#### Characteristics of a reasonably handled claim

Based on AFCA’s experience of general insurance complaints, a reasonably handled 
claim typically has the following characteristics:

• meaningful, clear, regular and consistent communication across all relevant 
stakeholders during the life of the claim, most importantly to the complainant, and 
where possible, with a consistent member of the insurer’s staff

• robust and flexible claims handling practices that can adapt to the specific 
characteristics of the claim (e.g. when a complainant is vulnerable)

• fair claim decisions that are supported by reasonable information shared with the 
complainant

• internal dispute resolution (IDR) practices that engage with the complainant, 
consider their specific concerns and provide clear reasons for the insurer’s 
decisions.

This is not an exhaustive list. There are also likely situations that cut across several of 
these factors. For example, an insurer undertaking:

• repairs - requires good communication, a robust and flexible claims handling 
process and fair claim decisions

• cash settlements - should not do so as a first option, requires good communication 
and should be supported by reasonable information.

A failure to meet any of the above may result in AFCA finding the insurer has failed to 
handle the claim reasonably.

#### Remedies for poorly handled claims

AFCA considers a failure to handle a claim properly a breach of the insurer’s 
obligation under the insurance policy. In those cases, AFCA will consider what fair 
remedies, if any, should be awarded.

The remedies considered will depend on the specific complaint and the outcomes the 
complainant is seeking. For example, compensation may be awarded for:

• the distress, inconvenience or interference to the complainant, caused by the poor 
claims handling

• financial losses such as hire car costs, temporary accommodation, loss of 
rent/income, and property damage being sought by the complainant.

The above is not an exhaustive list.

#### Non-financial loss

AFCA will commonly award non-financial loss if it is satisfied that:

• the insurer has handled the claim unreasonably, and

• this has resulted in the complainant suffering an unusual degree of interference, 
inconvenience or delay.

The amount of compensation will depend on the severity of the impact to the 
complainant. Compensation will only be considered if it exceeds the normal degree of 
inconvenience a person would experience when having a problem. Further details 
about our approach to awarding non-financial loss are set out in <u>AFCA&#39;s Approach to</u> 
<u>non-financial loss claims</u>.

#### Financial losses

AFCA may consider awarding financial losses if it is satisfied that the insurer has 
handled the claim unreasonably, and:

• this has resulted in the complainant suffering the financial loss claimed

• such a financial loss would have been reasonably foreseeable to the parties at the 
time the policy was issued/renewed, and

• the complainant has substantiated the amount being sought.

It is important to note that just because the insurer unreasonably handled the claim, 
this does not mean AFCA will award the financial losses sought. AFCA must be 
satisfied it is the insurer’s unreasonable claims handling that resulted in (or caused) 
the complainant to suffer the loss claimed. This can be difficult for the complainant to 
satisfy.

For example, a complainant who says they have incurred medical expenses will need 
to provide compelling evidence that it was this reason, and not other factors (such as 
a pre-existing condition or other personal matters), that caused them to incur these 
medical expenses. This can be challenging to prove.

In awarding financial loss, AFCA will consider if the loss is direct or indirect, given 
different monetary limits apply to each under the AFCA Rules. In general terms, direct 
loss flows naturally from the breach, while indirect loss occurs as a consequence of 
the breach but involves additional factors or events. This may be due to additional 
factors or events specific to the person’s circumstances. Therefore, what is direct or 
indirect loss depends on all the circumstances of the particular case.

In an insurance context, AFCA will generally consider whether the loss claimed is a 
normal loss that every person in a like situation (i.e. with the same contract) would be
likely to suffer. For example:

• an insured under a landlord policy seeking lost rent due to the insurer’s 
unreasonable delays would generally be considered direct loss – this is because 
most people with this type of policy would insure an investment property that 
generates rental income

• an insured under a home policy seeking lost rent due to the insurer’s unreasonable 
delays would generally be considered indirect – this is because most people with

Page **4** of **20** this type of policy would not suffer such a loss given they would typically use it for 
their place of residence.

In awarding financial losses, it is important to note any policy caps or limits would **not**
apply. For example, if an insurer’s poor repairs resulted in a home being a total loss 
or further damage to contents, it cannot apply the sum insured or policy limits to that 
loss.

The AFCA Rules note compensation for indirect financial loss cannot be provided if a 
general insurance policy expressly excludes this. However, AFCA does not accept 
this applies if the loss resulted from the insurer breaching its obligation under the 
policy. This is because such policy exclusions exclude these types of losses flowing 
from the claimed event under the policy, but not when the insurer failed to comply with 
its obligations.

#### Applying these principles to typical complaints

The next section of the Approach will set out how these principles of resolving claims 
fairly can be applied to common scenarios that AFCA deals with. These include:

• claim delays

• cash settlements in home claims

• claim denials involving expert reports.

#### 2.2 Claim delays

#### Common issues in claims delays

A common issue AFCA regularly deals with is complaints about claim delays. Any 
delays caused by an insurer would typically be considered a breach of its claims 
handling obligations. This would not include delays outside the insurer’s control (e.g. 
supply chain issues where the insurer has shown how they have affected the claim).

Delays can arise in numerous ways. Common delay complaints involve assertions 
that an insurer:

• is failing to make a claim decision within a reasonable time

• is failing to fairly assess the damage

• is taking too long in repairs

• is undertaking poor repairs.

If any of these assertions are substantiated, then it is likely AFCA will conclude the 
insurer has breached its claims handling obligations.

#### Compensation for delays in the insurer’s control

In considering these complaints, AFCA must be satisfied that:

• there were unreasonable delays, and

Page **5** of **20**

---

• these were caused by factors within the insurer’s control.

To the extent they were not, it is unlikely AFCA will accept the insurer breached its 
obligations. For instance:

• There have been recent issues securing trades and materials in the motor and 
home industries. This can result in claim delays that were outside the insurer’s 
control (although it is important the insurer show this was the cause and not other 
factors in its control).

• An insurer is normally entitled to investigate claims provided it does so reasonably 
and expeditiously. Delays may be experienced in a complainant co-operating with 
the insurer’s reasonable requests for information. This would generally not be 
considered within the insurer’s control.

AFCA has published a Fact Sheet and an Approach document that specifically set out 
how we approach certain types of situations. These can be found here:

#### • <u>Approach document: Motor vehicle insurance claim delays</u>

#### • <u>Fact Sheet: Home insurance claim delays and COVID-19</u>

As evident in those publications, it is important that an insurer acts fairly in 
progressing claims. This should include exploring reasonable options and alternatives 
to progressing claims if certain factors arise that interfere with its general process 
(e.g. usual suppliers not being able to source parts).

It is also important to note that an insurer will be considered responsible for the 
contractors and agents it appoints for a claim. Therefore, if any delays are caused by 
them, the insurer will generally be considered responsible for those delays.

For example, if a repairer engaged by the insurer caused additional damage or 
conducted poor repairs, then the insurer will be considered responsible for this.

#### Case study 1: Complainant must show loss caused by insurer<sup>2</sup>

The insurer accepted a claim for damage to the complainant’s vehicle. The insurer 
repaired the vehicle. However, it was damaged when it was towed back to the 
complainant. The insurer accepted this damage under a new claim.

The decision maker found the insurer’s approach to the repair costs was fair:

• the insurer had fairly assessed the cost of repairing the additional damage

• it was reasonable for the insurer to cash settle the complainant for the 
additional repairs, as the complainant maintained the insurer’s proposed repair 
method was inadequate without there being sufficient evidence to substantiate
this.

#### Remedies that may be awarded for claims delays

If AFCA accepts the insurer has caused claim delays, then a range of remedies can 
apply depending on the circumstances. For example:

• if the repairs to a vehicle require rectification, an insurer will typically be required to 
provide a hire car whilst those rectifications are undertaken even if the policy does 
not cover hire car costs

• if an insurer’s contractor causes additional damage, then the insurer will be liable 
for the full loss, regardless of whether the policy would not have covered the items 
or area, or a policy limit applied

• if the insurer has unduly delayed repairing an investment property, it can be 
required to pay lost rent even if that is not covered by the policy

• additional temporary accommodation costs (discussed further below).

Non-financial loss compensation would also often be considered. Interest may also be 
awarded to any financial remedy. AFCA typically awards interest under section 57 of 
the Act. Our general approach to this can be found in <u>AFCA&#39;s Approach to awarding</u> 
<u>interest in insurance</u>.

#### Case study 2: Insurer responsible for increased repair costs due to claim delay

The insurer authorised repairs for cyclone damage to the complainant’s home for 
around $100,000. Repairs had not started over 18 months later, when the insurer’s 
builder revised the repair cost to around $680,000. About $380,000 of the

Page **7** of **20** increased cost was due to the introduction of new cyclone-resistant building code 
requirements.

After considerable claim delays, the complainant did not want the insurer to 
complete the repairs. The insurer agreed but sought to limit its liability for the 
increased cost of complying with the new building code to $30,000, relying on a 
policy limit for building code compliance. The AFCA panel found the insurer was 
not entitled to limit its liability to the building sum insured amount or the code 
compliance benefit under the policy. This is because these limits were only 
exceeded due to the insurer’s delay in starting the repairs.

The insurer was required to cash settle the complainant based on the full cost of 
repairs, along with interest from the date the insurer made the inadequate 
settlement offer. The complainant was also awarded $6,300 compensation for the 
stress caused by the extended claim delay and the impact on her quality of life 
living in the damaged property for several years.

#### 2.3 Cash settlements in home building claims

#### Insurers must provide a fair cash settlement offer

AFCA regularly deals with complaints about an insurer’s cash settlement offer. In 
doing so, AFCA carefully considers the basis of the insurer’s offer to determine if it is 
fair in all the circumstances.

As set out previously, the insurer’s claims handling obligations include making fair 
claim decisions that are supported by relevant information shared with the 
complainant. This should include unredacted costings so the complainant can assess 
if the amount offered is fair.

If AFCA considers that the insurer has provided an unfair or inadequate cash 
settlement, then we will generally consider this is not aligned with the insurer’s claims 
handling obligation. In those circumstances we will consider alternative remedies,
such as requiring the insurer to carry out some or all of the repairs or increase the 
cash settlement offer, as discussed below.

#### Insurer must exercise discretion fairly

An insurer often has a discretion under the policy to either repair the damage, replace 
the item or pay the cost of either the repairs or replacement. However, this does not 
mean an insurer can unilaterally decide how to settle the claim.

When exercising a discretion under the policy, the insurer must do so fairly and 
consistently with the principles of utmost good faith.

If the insurer decides to cash settle a claim, AFCA will assess whether its reasons for 
doing so are consistent with the above principles. If they are not, then AFCA can find

Page **8** of **20** that the insurer must settle the claim in a different way which is fair in the particular 
circumstances.

For example, an insurer may want to cash settle a large home building claim where 
the complainant is vulnerable and unlikely to be able to deal with or manage the 
repair process. This may not be fair because the logistics involved in the repairs are 
complex and overwhelming. At the same time, by cash settling, the insurer is not 
providing a guarantee for the works, and the risk passes to the complainant. In such a 
situation, AFCA may decide to direct the insurer to complete the repairs.

In some cases, there may be a reasonable basis for the insurer to cash settle a claim. 
For instance, if there are pre-existing issues that need to be addressed by the 
complainant before the insured repairs can be done (e.g. fix a roof which is not 
covered by the policy, before repairing internal water damage which is covered by the 
policy).

Once an insurer has exercised its discretion, it will generally be bound by that. That is, 
it cannot choose to cash settle if it has begun repairs. However, exceptions may arise 
when it is neither practical nor feasible to continue (e.g. a home can no longer be 
repaired, there is a breakdown in the parties’ relationship, or the costs will likely 
exceed the sum insured).

#### Case study 3: Insurer did not fairly decide to cash settle vulnerable complainant

The insurer accepted the complainant’s claim for flood damage to his home and 
appointed a builder to complete the repairs. The complainant told the builder the 
property contained asbestos. The builder did not follow the assessor’s instructions 
to safely remove the asbestos, contaminating the entire home.

The insurer then sought to cash settle the claim (including the asbestos 
remediation). It said the complainant was physically aggressive with the builder.

The complainant acknowledged becoming frustrated due to poor communication 
and delays by the insurer and angry about the asbestos contamination and 
incorrect safety certificate issued by the builder. He denied assaulting the builder.

The AFCA panel accepted the complainant used inappropriate language, and the 
insurer had an obligation to provide a safe working environment for its staff and 
contractors. It acknowledged the seriousness of the assault allegation, but noted 
this was only supported by a comment in an email from one of the builder’s 
employees. The insurer had not spoken with the builder involved, interviewed the 
complainant, considered any incident report, or otherwise investigated the 
allegation.

The complainant told the insurer he had a medical condition that made it difficult for 
him to deal with people. The insurer did not record this in its claim notes or take 
any steps to accommodate the disclosed condition, including in its review of the

Page **9** of **20** complainant’s conduct. The AFCA panel considered the insurer did not give the 
complainant any real opportunity to respond to the allegations before it decided to 
cash settle the claim.

The AFCA panel found the insurer failed to consider the complainant’s condition in 
its management of the claim, and noted the complainant had reasonable grounds 
to be frustrated due to the poor claim handling. The AFCA panel did not consider it 
was fair in the circumstances to cash settle the claim however, it did require:

• the complainant to appoint a representative to deal with the insurer and its 
contactors on his behalf

• the insurer to appoint a dedicated claim manager with experience with the 
complainant’s circumstances as a person with a disability

• the insurer to provide a direct phone number for the claim manager to the 
representative, and to provide fortnightly updates and copies of all assessment 
and expert reports

• the parties to work together to enable the repairs

• the insurer to pay the complainant’s temporary accommodation costs until the 
house was liveable, due to the contamination negligently caused by the builder.

The complainant had engaged a lawyer to help him deal with the insurer. The 
panel considered this was reasonable in the circumstances, particularly given the 
complainant’s disability. The Panel accepted it was reasonable for the insurer to 
reimburse the complainant for these costs and pay him $5,400 non-financial loss 
compensation for major stress and inconvenience.

#### Characteristics of a good cash settlement offer

If there is no dispute about the insurer’s decision to cash settle, or AFCA agrees that 
the insurer’s decision to cash settle is fair, then AFCA considers whether the cash 
settlement amount offered is fair.

These are the main things AFCA will generally consider in any cash settlement:

• the policy wording

• the scope of works any quote is based on

• the repair quotes

• the inclusion of any contingency (this is particularly important noting the transfer of 
risk)

• if the cash settlement includes all relevant benefits under the policy.

#### Scope of works

The scope of works is generally the most important factor. AFCA must be satisfied the 
scope of works fairly reflects the repairs necessary for the claim related damage. This 
includes whether it is consistent with the policy (e.g. to restore the property back to its 
condition when new, or identifies all claim related damage). To the extent the scope of

Page **10** of **20** works does not, then it is unlikely any quotation based on such a scope of works 
would be considered fair.

A fair scope of works should at least include the following:

• a reasonable description of the materials and labour required including any scope 
for customisation and how the work meets the insured’s personal needs

• a reasonable description of all the works needed to repair the claim related 
damage

• whether the insured can be present during the building works or requires 
alternative accommodation

• a reasonable estimate of the time required to conduct the work

• steps taken to secure the property during the work including the insured’s 
possessions and any materials obtained for the purpose of repairs

• contact details of the person responsible for the day-to-day management of the 
work and with whom feedback or concerns should be raised.

#### Quotation

The next most important factor is the quotation itself. A fair quotation would typically:

• be based on a fair or established scope of works

• be detailed and itemised

• be actionable to the complainant

• include a builder’s margin.

To the extent the quote does not, then it is less likely AFCA will consider the quote is 
fair.

#### Contingency

It is relatively common for the final cost of an insurance claim to be higher than the 
initial quotes, particularly home insurance claims. This is because, after repairs start, 
further issues or other costs can be identified. In particular, the more complex or 
uncertain the extent of the works are, the more likely it is that this will happen.

As a cash settlement transfers this risk to the complainant, AFCA will generally apply 
a contingency to any cash settlement that is based on a quote. This is to mitigate that 
risk as well as take into account that the complainant will not have the benefit of the 
insurer being responsible for the quality of the work and the materials used.

We will apply a contingency of between 5-25% but often between 10-20%. Generally, 
the higher the risk of there being additional work or costs, the higher the contingency 
will be. In some unusual cases, it may exceed 25% but this is rare. If the insurer’s 
offer includes a contingency in its offer, AFCA will consider whether the percentage is 
fair having regard to the above.

#### Other policy benefits

It is also important that any cash settlement includes any other policy benefits. This 
may include benefits such as temporary accommodation, removal of debris and 
professional fees (amongst others). These benefits should be considered regardless 
of whether the complainant explicitly requests them or not, if the information shows 
they are likely to be applicable.

#### Additional costs

#### Temporary accommodation costs

When a cash settlement dispute arises where the home is unliveable, the complainant 
is typically incurring temporary accommodation costs. However, most policies contain 
monetary and/or time limits on temporary accommodation benefits. These can often 
be exhausted when a dispute arises about the underlying claims settlement.

Therefore, if AFCA accepts the complainant has shown the insurer’s settlement is 
unfair, it can award temporary accommodation costs beyond the policy limits. That is, 
AFCA would likely find that any temporary accommodation costs incurred by a 
complainant whilst disputing the insurer’s offer would be financial losses flowing from 
the insurer’s poor claims handling, not the claim itself. As a result, it would not be 
subject to the policy limits.

#### Professional costs

A complainant can incur professional costs in challenging the insurer’s settlement 
offer. Those costs would involve the engagement of experts (e.g. engineers and/or 
builders) and/or professional representatives.

AFCA Rule D.5 provides that a decision-maker may decide the insurer is to contribute 
to the complainant’s legal or professional costs.

AFCA’s service is free, and it is not usually necessary for either party to be 
professionally represented. So, there is no automatic right to recouping professional 
costs, even if the complainant is otherwise successful in the complaint outcome.

Generally, AFCA considers awarding professional costs if the complainant needed to 
incur them to understand or establish their claim. AFCA may award professional costs 
in the following circumstances:

• the main issue in dispute was the cause of the disputed damage, and the 
complainant engaged an expert report that substantially helped the decision-maker 
to fairly determine this issue

• the complainant was vulnerable and unable to fairly advocate their position, and 
the representative materially helped the complainant to receive an outcome in their 
favour

Page **12** of **20**

---

• the complaint raised legal issues which the complainant reasonably needed advice 
and help on.

AFCA will not award such costs if AFCA is not satisfied the complainant needed to 
incur them. For instance, if AFCA is not satisfied the complainant required the help of 
a professional representative, such as a lawyer or paid representative or (noting 
AFCA is a free service), then such costs will not be awarded. AFCA will generally not 
factor in any of a paid representative’s (such as a claims manager’s) fees when 
considering a fair outcome.

Costs that are awarded are usually capped at $5,000 per complaint unless special 
circumstances apply. Special circumstances may apply where:

• the issue in dispute was particularly complex (e.g. required multiple expert reports)
• the complaint involved complex legal issues in areas where there is uncertainty in 
the law.

Special circumstances are rarely applied.

#### Case study 4: Delayed settlement caused additional accommodation costs

The insurer decided to cash settle the complainant’s claim for storm damage to her 
home. However, it did not make any payment or complete restoration work for 18 
months, preventing the complainant from starting the repairs. The complainant 
engaged her own building consultant to determine the full scope and cost of repairs 
after several attempts by the insurer. The insurer’s builder recommended the 
insurer settle the complainant on this basis, and this was also accepted by the 
AFCA panel.

The policy provided cover for reasonable temporary accommodation costs for the 
time it would take to repair the property to a liveable condition, limited to the lesser 
of 12 months or 10% of the sum insured. The insurer paid the complainant the 
maximum policy benefit for temporary accommodation. However, the complainant 
was unable to repair or return to the property in the period specified by this benefit 
as the insurer mishandled the claim. The AFCA panel determined it was fair for the 
insurer to pay for additional temporary accommodation costs incurred by the 
complainant beyond the policy limit until the repairs could reasonably be 
completed, subject to proof of these costs.

The insurer was also required to reimburse the complainant $5,000 towards her 
building consultant’s fees, as its reports were relied on to determine the insurer’s 
liability. This is the maximum AFCA can generally award for professional costs 
(unless special circumstances apply). The complainant was also awarded $5,400 
compensation for non-financial loss (the maximum amount AFCA could award at 
the time) for avoidable stress and inconvenience caused by the mismanagement of

Page **13** of **20** the claim. This mismanagement resulted in the complainant being unable to live in 
her home for an extended period.

#### Types of remedies that may be awarded if the offer is unfair

If AFCA concludes the cash settlement is unfair, then it will seek to calculate a fair 
settlement based on the exchanged information. If it cannot, then AFCA will typically 
set out specific directions for the parties to follow to calculate a fair settlement.

Further, AFCA will consider any remedies available to the complainant as a result of 
the insurer’s failure to comply with its claims handling obligations. This can include 
non-financial loss or other financial losses as described above, as well as 
professional costs the complainant has incurred.

#### Case study 5: Insurer’s inadequate make safe caused additional damage

The complainants lodged a claim for stormwater ingress to their home, which the 
insurer accepted and decided to cash settle. The insurer arranged a make safe 
review of the property, during which mould was noted, and prompt remediation was 
recommended to avoid further growth. There was also a concern about mosquitoes 
in the house due to saturated carpets. Around a month later, the property was 
found to no longer be habitable with no containment steps having been taken to 
prevent further loss or make the property safe. Despite this, the complainants were 
not promptly moved to temporary accommodation.

The AFCA panel noted complainants have a responsibility to mitigate their loss. 
However, the AFCA panel acknowledged the complainants had sought guidance 
from the insurer about what steps to take to minimise further damage, and took 
available steps, including removing carpets, cleaning and trying to salvage 
contents items.

The AFCA panel considered no reasonable make safe measures were taken by 
the insurer to protect the complainants’ health. The insurer failed to act on the 
recommendations made by its consultants, allowing mould to spread through the 
home. As a result, the AFCA panel found the insurer was not entitled to rely on the 
policy terms to limit its liability for the additional damage, and was responsible for 
the full extent of this loss. Additionally, the panel awarded $6,300 compensation for 
non-financial loss to each of the complainants, whose health was impacted during 
the claim.

#### 2.4 Claim denials – expert evidence

One of the main issues dealt with by AFCA in general insurance complaints involves 
claim denials. In many of these complaints, expert evidence is being relied upon by 
the insurer in denying the claim.

---

Part of an insurer’s claims handling obligation is to make fair decisions supported by 
relevant information. If AFCA concludes the insurer’s expert evidence provided little or 
no reasonable basis for the opinion, then it may also be considered a breach of the 
insurer’s claims handling obligation, if it relied upon this evidence to deny a claim.

In such a case, AFCA would consider any remedies that flow from such a failure, as 
set out above. This would likely include non-financial loss, any financial loss 
substantiated and professional costs.

Even if the expert evidence did provide some reasonable basis for the opinion but this 
was ultimately not accepted by AFCA (e.g. the complainant provided a more 
persuasive report during the complaints process), the insurer would still be 
considered to have breached its obligations under the policy. This is because the 
insurer has denied the claim in circumstances that cannot be substantiated in 
weighing up the competing information.

In such a case, financial losses sustained by the complainant by that breach (e.g. 
additional temporary accommodation beyond the policy limit) would be considered. 
However, it is unlikely non-financial loss would be awarded. This is because an 
insurer who has denied a claim in such circumstances is unlikely to have breached its 
claims handling obligations. This is due to the fact it did rely on relevant information, 
albeit it was not persuasive enough to satisfy an independent decision-maker.

#### AFCA’s consideration of expert reports

If a factual issue requires the assistance of an expert, then AFCA’s usual approach is 
to evaluate the expert’s evidence. Examples of this will be where the issue requires 
the assistance of a person with specialised knowledge in the subject matter (e.g. 
hydrologists for flood, engineers for ground movement damage, health practitioners 
for medical issues).

When evaluating an expert opinion, there are a range of factors AFCA considers. 
These include the facts, matters or assumptions the opinion is based on, as well as 
whether the expert:

• is independent

• is qualified to provide an opinion on the factual issue

• has considered all relevant information

• has considered irrelevant or incorrect information

• opinion is plausible, logical and consistent with the available facts.

#### Case study 6: Reliance on inadequate information to decline a claim

The complainant lodged a claim for damage to his vehicle. There was no dispute 
the vehicle was damaged unintentionally. This met the policy requirements for an 
accidental damage claim. The insurer referred to policy exclusions for wear and

Page **15** of **20** tear, mechanical failure and failure to maintain the vehicle. However, these factors 
were not addressed in its expert reports.

The decision maker said the insurer had incorrectly placed the onus on the 
complainant to show the exclusions did not apply. The decision maker considered 
the information provided was insufficient to show, on balance, the damage was due 
to an excluded cause. The insurer was required to accept the claim under the 
policy terms. The insurer was also required to pay interest if it elected to cash 
settle the claim and pay the complainant $1,100 compensation for non-financial 
loss, due to unreasonably declining the claim based on inadequate information.

#### The characteristics of a good expert report

A good expert report will confirm the facts, matters and assumptions on which each 
opinion expressed in the report is based. The report should:

• have considered all relevant matters (e.g. a hydrologist should have considered 
available data and any eyewitness evidence)

• not base any opinion on matters that cannot be substantiated, are incorrect (e.g. 
an engineer saying a property is built on reactive clay when it is not) or irrelevant

• include conclusions that logically follow the observations made by the expert and 
the available facts

• address any obvious issues

• not provide an opinion on matters outside the expert’s expertise (e.g. whether the 
policy should respond or not).

Importantly, a good expert opinion should provide a plausible, logical explanation 
supporting the opinion, that a non-technical person can readily understand.

#### How AFCA deals with conflicting reports

When weighing up conflicting expert reports, AFCA considers the following factors:

• the facts, observations or part conclusions the experts agree upon (e.g. the experts 
may agree that a building is damaged and the type of damage which has occurred 
but not how the damage was caused)

• their respective qualifications (e.g. a structural engineer regarding the cause of 
ground movement would generally be better qualified than a builder on such an 
issue)

• whether one report has considered more relevant information

• if one of the reports relied on incorrect assumptions or inaccurate information

• if either of the reports explain why they consider the conclusions of the other is 
incorrect and whether that explanation is persuasive

• whether one expert viewed the damage closer to the time of the event

• if one expert made more appropriate enquiries

• if one expert had the benefit of reviewing any prior expert reports

• if the expert provided an opinion based on a desktop assessment or a physical 
examination and to what extent this would have made a difference.

Not all these factors will be applicable. However, often it will be a combination of 
several factors in this list which leads AFCA to prefer one expert report over the other.

#### What outcome would AFCA consider if no expert report is accepted

If AFCA does not find either expert report compelling, then AFCA will consider which 
party has the onus of proof and how it impacts the claim. For example, if the issue is 
part of the complainant’s obligation to establish a claim (e.g. to show the damage was 
caused by an insured event), then the insurer would generally be able to deny the 
claim.

Alternatively, if the issue relates to an exclusion (e.g. wear and tear), and this is the 
only ground raised by the insurer, then the insurer will likely have to pay the claim.

#### When AFCA may seek an expert report

In very limited circumstances, AFCA can seek to obtain an expert report. This is set 
out in Rule A.9.7. This would likely only occur where:

• there is a complex technical issue in dispute that requires determination

• there is either no expert opinion or, more commonly, both parties have provided 
expert opinions but none of them provide any meaningful input to the issue in 
question

• this is the fairest option to resolve the complaint in all the circumstances.

AFCA will rarely do this. This is due to various reasons:

• most insurance complaints at AFCA have expert reports

• the issue can often be determined based on the available expert evidence, or 
which party has the onus of proof (e.g. if the insurer has the onus to prove an 
exclusion, and the expert evidence is insufficient, AFCA will likely decide in the 
complainant’s favour),

• this can significantly prolong the dispute resolution process (e.g. by months) 
because:

> it will take time to find, identify and engage the expert

> the parties must then wait for the expert to complete the report, and

> the expert report will need to be exchanged, and each party be given a 
reasonable opportunity to comment on it.

AFCA is not bound by the findings of the expert opinion – this is because a decisionmaker must still independently evaluate the report, along with all the other exchanged 
information, to decide whether they are persuaded by the opinion or not.

#### AFCA can reimburse expert report costs in limited circumstances

AFCA can reimburse the cost of the complainant’s expert report. This would normally 
happen when:

• the issue required the assistance of an expert

• the expert report assists our consideration of the matter

• the report supported the complainant’s position.

We limit this to $5,000 per complaint unless special circumstances apply.

### 4 Appendix

#### 4.1 AFCA’s purpose and jurisdiction

#### AFCA’s purpose

AFCA is the independent external dispute resolution (EDR) scheme for the financial
services sector. AFCA’s purpose is to provide fair, independent, and effective
solutions for financial disputes. We do this by providing fair dispute resolution
services. We also work with financial firms to improve their processes and standards
of service to minimise future complaints. In addition to resolving financial complaints,
AFCA identifies, resolves, and reports on systemic issues and serious contraventions
of the law.

#### Complaints AFCA can consider

It is important to understand that each AFCA complaint has a unique set of facts and
this information is a guide only. We will always consider the nature, size and scale of
a complaint and the impact of issues on all parties.

AFCA can only consider complaints against financial firms that are members of
AFCA, provided the complaints meet the other requirements in <u>our Rules</u> (for
example Rule A.4 – Complaints AFCA can consider).

#### AFCA’s fairness jurisdiction

Our decisions are intended to reflect what is fair in the circumstances of each
complaint. This includes providing a fair outcome where we find the financial firm
made an error or breached an obligation to the complainant.

In assessing what is fair, we apply a standard of fairness which focuses on concepts
such as fair dealing, fair treatment and fair service. We may consider the conduct of
both parties when determining a fair outcome, and we will consider all the
circumstances to determine an outcome that is fair to the parties.
