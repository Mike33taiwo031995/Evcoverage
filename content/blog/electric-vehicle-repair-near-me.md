Finding electric vehicle repair near you is harder than finding an ordinary mechanic. An EV has a high-voltage battery, power electronics, an electric drive unit and a lot of software, and not every shop has the training or equipment to work on them safely. Searching "electric vehicle repair near me" will show you plenty of businesses, but a listing alone does not tell you whether a shop can handle your car's problem.

The good news is that not every EV repair needs a specialist. Tires, suspension and many 12-volt electrical jobs can be handled by a well-equipped general shop. The line to draw is high-voltage work, where the choice of technician matters for your safety and your wallet.

This guide explains how to find qualified EV repair shops, which directories are worth using, what questions to ask, how dealers and independents compare, and what to prepare before you book. We cannot list specific shops for your town, so the focus is on how to check any shop you find.

**Short answer:** Use your manufacturer's service network, the ASE Blue Seal and AAA Approved Auto Repair locators, and local searches to build a shortlist, then call each shop and ask about high-voltage training and experience with your exact model. NHTSA says a qualified technician with EV-specific high-voltage training should service your EV.

## What counts as EV repair?

EV repair covers the mechanical, electrical, electronic and software systems that let an electric vehicle run. Depending on the car and the fault, that can include:

- High-voltage battery diagnostics
- Electric motor and drive-unit inspection
- Inverter and power electronics servicing
- Charging-port and onboard charging repairs
- 12-volt battery replacement
- Wiring and connector repairs
- Brake, suspension, tire and alignment work
- Heating, air-conditioning and thermal management repairs
- Software diagnostics and updates
- Collision assessment and repair

### Which jobs need a specialist?

NHTSA's EV guidance is direct: it says a qualified technician with specialized, EV-specific high-voltage training should service your electric vehicle, with the right protective equipment and testing or diagnostic tools, and warns that severe injury or death may result if an unqualified technician attempts the work. It also says you should never try to service the traction battery yourself without proper training and equipment ([NHTSA](https://www.nhtsa.gov/vehicle-safety/electric-and-hybrid-vehicles)).

NHTSA also draws a useful distinction between the two batteries: the high-voltage battery cannot be jump-started, but in most cases the 12-volt battery, which powers lighting and instrumentation, can be jumped like that of a gasoline car. Routine work such as tires, many brake jobs and standard 12-volt electrical repairs is generally closer to ordinary shop work, but always confirm the shop's experience with your model.

For more on the electrical side, including the 12-volt system, see our guide to [vehicle electrical repair](/blog/vehicle-electrical-repair/).

## How to find electric vehicle repair shops near you

### Search Google and Google Maps, then check the shop

Searches such as "EV mechanic near me," "electric car repair shop near me," "Tesla repair near me" or "hybrid and electric vehicle repair near me" will give you a first list, with addresses, directions and reviews. Treat it only as a starting point. A listing does not prove a business is qualified for high-voltage work, so read its services page, look for named EV brands and training, and read reviews that mention EV repairs specifically.

### Use the ASE Blue Seal program

The National Institute for Automotive Service Excellence (ASE) runs the Blue Seal of Excellence recognition program for repair businesses. To qualify, at least 75% of the technicians who diagnose and repair vehicles must be ASE certified, and each area of service must be covered by at least one certified technician ([ASE Blue Seal program](https://ase.com/blue-seal-program)). ASE also provides an online locator for Blue Seal shops through its website.

ASE certification shows assessed general knowledge. It does not by itself prove that a shop can do every EV repair, so still ask about high-voltage experience.

### Use AAA's Approved Auto Repair locator

AAA operates an Approved Auto Repair network in the U.S. and Canada, and says its approved facilities are required to have ASE-certified or OEM-trained technicians ([AAA](https://ww1.aaa.com/autorepair/articles/how-do-you-choose-the-right-auto-repair-shop)). AAA says you do not need to be a member to use these shops, although members may be eligible for extra benefits ([AAA Club Alliance](https://cluballiance.aaa.com/the-extra-mile/advice/car/do-you-know-how-to-find-a-reputable-repair-shop)). As with ASE, approval does not guarantee that a particular location does high-voltage battery work, so call and confirm.

### Check your manufacturer's service network

Most manufacturers list authorized service centers on their website or app, including Tesla, Ford, Chevrolet, Hyundai, Kia, Rivian, BMW and Nissan. These centers are often the best choice for warranty work, software problems and repairs that need manufacturer-specific procedures. Availability varies by brand and area, so check how far the nearest authorized center is before you assume it is convenient.

### Look for independent EV specialists

Independent EV shops can be a good option, especially once the factory warranty has ended. Some focus on one brand such as Tesla, while others work on several. Before you book, confirm exactly which models and systems they service.

### If you are outside the U.S. or Canada

ASE and AAA are North American. Elsewhere, look for your own country's recognized qualification for hybrid and electric vehicle technicians, and ask the shop which one its technicians hold. Ford's workshop information, for example, states that service of its high-voltage systems is restricted to qualified personnel, that required qualifications vary by region, and that local laws on EV service apply ([I-CAR](https://rts.i-car.com/crn-1677.html)).

## What EV repairs can you get near you?

What a shop can do depends on its equipment, its technicians and the manufacturer. Here are the main categories.

### Traction battery repair

The traction battery stores the energy that powers the motor and is managed by monitoring and control electronics. Problems can involve the battery-management system, cooling, enclosure damage, electrical connections, charging behavior, reduced capacity or damage from collisions or flooding. A technician must find the real cause first, and not every problem means replacing the whole pack, because some designs allow a smaller component to be repaired under the manufacturer's approved procedures. Ask whether the shop does battery-level diagnostics or only swaps complete packs.

For cost and coverage questions, read [how much EV battery replacement costs in 2026](/blog/ev-battery-replacement-cost-2026/) and [how battery warranty compares with insurance](/blog/ev-battery-warranty-vs-insurance/).

### Electric motor and drive-unit repair

Motors have fewer moving parts than combustion engines, but they can still fail. Symptoms include unusual noises, reduced acceleration, vibration, warning messages and drive-system fault codes. Because the inverter, wiring, software and drivetrain can cause similar symptoms, accurate diagnosis comes before any parts are ordered.

### Charging system repair

Charging complaints include a car that does not recognize the connector, stops charging unexpectedly, charges slowly or shows an electrical warning. The cause could be the connector, charging port, onboard charger, the electrical supply, communication between car and charger, or software. It is important to find out whether the fault is in the vehicle or in the charging station or home wiring. If the problem is at home, see [whether home insurance covers an EV charger](/blog/does-home-insurance-cover-ev-charger/).

### Brakes

Electric vehicles use regenerative braking, but it complements friction brakes and does not replace them. Industry trade coverage notes that EVs and gasoline cars both rely on friction brakes with the same basic parts (rotors, calipers and pads), though regeneration can reduce wear ([Babcox Media](https://www.bodyshopbusiness.com/?p=224391)). You still need the brakes inspected on schedule, and hydraulic, caliper, fluid and parking-brake problems still occur.

### Suspension, steering and tires

Because EVs carry heavy battery packs, suspension and tires work hard. Common jobs include shock absorbers, control arms, ball joints, steering components, alignment and tire replacement. Arrange an inspection if you hear new noises, feel vibration or see uneven tire wear. General shops often handle this work, but confirm they have experience with your model.

### Heating, air conditioning and thermal management

Many EVs use coolant circuits, pumps, valves and heat exchangers to regulate battery and cabin temperature. A fault can affect heating, cooling, charging performance or battery temperature, and these systems can be tied into battery cooling, so they may need specialized diagnosis.

### Collision repair

After an accident, ask whether the body shop has experience with EVs and whether it follows the manufacturer's repair procedures. NHTSA's guidance for owners after damage or fire says to assume the high-voltage battery and components are energized, not to attempt repairs yourself, and to contact an authorized service center or the manufacturer as soon as possible ([NHTSA interim guidance](https://www.nhtsa.gov/sites/nhtsa.gov/files/interimguide_consumers_011912_v2a.pdf)). If the damage is serious enough to threaten a write-off, read our guide on [EV battery damage, total loss and GAP coverage](/blog/ev-battery-total-loss-gap-coverage/).

## How much does EV repair cost near you?

There is no single price. Cost depends on the vehicle, the repair type, parts availability, labor rates, diagnostic time and your location. We have not found a reliable published national price for most EV-specific repairs, such as charging-port, onboard-charger or high-voltage battery work, so we do not quote any.

| Repair type | What affects the cost |
|---|---|
| EV diagnostics | Technician experience, diagnostic time, equipment |
| 12-volt battery replacement | Battery specification, installation, vehicle requirements |
| Tires | Size, brand, specification |
| Brakes | Component condition, parts, labor |
| Suspension | Damage severity, design, replacement parts |
| Charging-port repair | Fault location, parts, diagnostic complexity |
| Onboard charger | Component availability, repair procedure |
| Battery cooling system | Pumps, valves, coolant components, labor |
| Electric motor | Fault severity, parts availability |
| High-voltage battery | Battery design, approved repair methods, specialist labor |

Prices differ between places because labor rates, technician availability, parts delivery times, shop costs and manufacturer requirements all vary. For example, a big-city shop can have different rates from one in a small town.

### Should you choose the cheapest shop?

Not automatically. A low estimate is only a bargain if the diagnosis is right and the repair fixes the cause. Ask the shop to explain what is wrong, which parts need attention and why. Request an itemized estimate that separates diagnosis, parts, labor and extras, and consider a second opinion on an expensive repair. Our guide to [vehicle electrical repair costs](/blog/vehicle-electrical-repair-cost/) shows published U.S. estimates for diagnosis, starters and alternators and explains how to read a quote and your rights to a written estimate.

## Dealer or independent EV shop: which is better?

Both can be right. Which one fits depends on the problem.

| Consideration | Authorized service center | Independent EV specialist |
|---|---|---|
| Manufacturer-specific knowledge | Often strong | Varies by training and experience |
| Warranty work | Usually the straightforward route | Depends on the warranty and repair |
| Software access | May have manufacturer tools | Varies by brand and access |
| High-voltage repair | Depends on the facility's qualifications | Depends on the facility's qualifications |
| Parts | Often manufacturer-specified | May offer approved alternatives |
| Price | Varies by manufacturer and location | Varies by business and repair |

### When an authorized center makes sense

Choose it when the car is still under a relevant warranty, when the fault involves manufacturer-specific software, when the manufacturer requires a specific repair procedure, or when the battery or drivetrain fault is complex.

### When an independent shop may suit you

Consider an independent shop for routine maintenance, repairs outside the warranty period, a second diagnostic opinion, or when the business has proven experience with your model and the right equipment.

### Will an independent repair void your warranty?

In the United States, the FTC says the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act makes it illegal for manufacturers or dealers to void your warranty or deny coverage simply because you used an independent repairer or an aftermarket part ([FTC consumer alert](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-FT-PURL-gpo18134/pdf/GOVPUB-FT-PURL-gpo18134.pdf)). That does not mean anything goes. A manufacturer can still refuse to cover a failure if it shows that the independent repair or part caused it. Keep invoices and records of all work. Rules on warranties differ outside the U.S.

## How to tell whether an EV shop is qualified

### Check technician certifications

ASE offers the Light Duty Hybrid/Electric Vehicle Specialist (L3) certification. According to ASE, the L3 test covers diagnosis of the battery system, drive system, power electronics and hybrid supporting systems, and candidates must first pass ASE's A6 and A8 tests ([ASE](https://ase.com/?p=2674)). Ask whether any technician at the shop holds it, along with any manufacturer EV training. A certificate is evidence of assessed knowledge, not a guarantee that the shop can do every EV repair.

### Ask about high-voltage safety training

A qualified shop should understand isolation procedures, protective equipment, testing methods and the manufacturer's safety requirements. High-voltage cabling is usually orange, and technicians should not work on it without proper training, service information and support, according to subject-matter experts quoted by Fleet Maintenance magazine ([Fleet Maintenance](https://www.fleetmaintenance.com/in-the-bay/diagnostic-and-repair/article/10978524/dont-let-your-technicians-get-fried)).

### Confirm experience with your model

A shop that works on one brand may not know another. Ask these questions:

1. Do you service my make, model and year?
2. Have you diagnosed this fault before?
3. Do you have the diagnostic equipment and software access for it?
4. Can you do the whole repair here, or will it be sent elsewhere?
5. Do you follow the manufacturer's repair procedures?
6. What warranty do you give on parts and labor?

The answers will quickly separate real EV capability from a general shop that is learning on your car.

## Signs your EV needs professional repair

### Reduced driving range

A sudden drop in range can come from cold or hot weather, tire pressure, heating or air-conditioning use, driving conditions, software or energy-management issues, or a battery fault. A lower displayed range does not automatically mean a defective battery, so a technician can help tell normal variation from a real problem. If you are worried about gradual capacity loss, see our guide on whether insurance covers EV battery degradation.

### Charging interruptions

If charging repeatedly stops or the car does not recognize the connection, have both the car and the charging equipment checked. The fault could be in the station, the supply, the connector, the port, the onboard charger or the software.

### Dashboard warnings

Do not ignore persistent warnings about the battery, drivetrain, charging system or electrical components. Check the owner's manual, and if the car says it should not be driven, stop and arrange assistance.

### Smoke, strong smells or heat

Smoke, unusual heat or signs of battery damage need immediate action. Move away from the vehicle and call emergency services if there is smoke, fire or an immediate danger. Never try to open or inspect a damaged high-voltage battery.

### Unusual noises or vibration

EVs are quieter than gasoline cars, but grinding, knocking, vibration or drivetrain noise still deserves an inspection.

## What to do before you take your EV in

1. **Write down the symptoms.** Note when the problem started, whether it happens while charging or driving, and whether it is constant.
2. **Photograph warning messages** when it is safe to do so.
3. **Note the vehicle details:** make, model, year and approximate mileage.
4. **Check your coverage.** Find out whether a manufacturer warranty, service plan or insurance policy might apply.
5. **Call first.** Describe the problem, confirm the shop can handle it, and ask about diagnostic fees and timing before arranging transport.
6. **Get the estimate in writing** and keep your invoices.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can any mechanic work on an electric car?**
Some work, such as tires, many brake jobs and standard 12-volt electrical repairs, can be done by an equipped general shop. NHTSA says a qualified technician with EV-specific high-voltage training should service your EV, so choose accordingly for anything involving high-voltage components.

**How do I find a good EV mechanic near me?**
Build a shortlist from your manufacturer's service network, the ASE Blue Seal and AAA Approved Auto Repair locators, and local searches. Then call each shop and ask about high-voltage training and experience with your model.

**Does AAA Approved or ASE Blue Seal mean a shop can fix my EV?**
No, not on its own. Both signal that the shop employs certified or trained technicians, but you should still confirm that a particular location does the high-voltage work you need.

**Will using an independent shop void my EV warranty?**
In the U.S., the FTC says warranty coverage cannot be denied or voided simply because you used an independent shop or aftermarket part. Coverage can still be refused if the independent work or part caused the failure. Keep your records.

**Can I jump-start an electric car?**
NHTSA says the high-voltage battery cannot be jumped, but the 12-volt battery usually can be, much like in a gasoline car. Follow your owner's manual.

**Who should repair a damaged EV battery?**
A technician with high-voltage training and the manufacturer's procedures. NHTSA tells owners not to attempt repairs on a damaged EV themselves and to contact an authorized service center or the manufacturer.

**Is a dealer or an independent shop cheaper?**
It depends on the shop, the repair and your location, so compare itemized written estimates for the same work.
