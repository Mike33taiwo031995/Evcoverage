Two drivers can own the same electric car and pay very different amounts to charge it. One charges at home overnight. The other relies on public stations with higher rates and occasional parking fees.

Neither bill can be understood from the battery size alone.

**The simplest useful calculation is energy purchased multiplied by the price per unit.** For a monthly estimate, combine your mileage, the car's energy use and the share of charging you expect to do at each price.

This guide uses clearly labeled examples in U.S. dollars. They are calculations, not current national tariff averages or guaranteed running costs. Substitute your local currency and rates; the arithmetic is the same.

## The two units you need to recognize

A kilowatt, or kW, measures power: how quickly energy is delivered at a particular moment. A kilowatt-hour, or kWh, measures energy.

When a station charges per kWh, the energy figure determines the basic bill. A charger's advertised maximum power is not the amount of energy you buy and is not a promise that your car will accept that power throughout the session.

The [U.S. Department of Energy and EPA charging guide](https://www.fueleconomy.gov/feg/charging.shtml) explains that charging costs depend on electricity prices and vehicle efficiency, and that public pricing may use energy, time or other charging arrangements.

For a simple bill check:

**Charging energy cost = billed kWh × price per kWh.**

Then add any applicable session, parking, idle or other fees and taxes.

## A transparent cost table

Assume, only for this example, that the car requires **30 kWh purchased per 100 miles**. That is an illustrative wall-energy figure, not a claim about every EV.

| Electricity price | Energy cost per 100 miles | Energy cost per 1,000 miles |
|---|---|---|
| $0.15/kWh | $4.50 | $45 |
| $0.25/kWh | $7.50 | $75 |
| $0.50/kWh | $15.00 | $150 |

The table excludes subscriptions, installation, parking, idle fees and other charges. Its purpose is to show how strongly the tariff changes the answer without changing the car.

At 1,000 miles, the assumed energy purchase is 300 kWh. Multiply that by your actual rate for a more relevant starting point than an online claim that charging costs one fixed amount.

## What does a single home charge cost?

{{< editorial-image name="ev-home-charging" alt="Illustration of an electric crossover connected to a wall-mounted charger at a dry residential driveway" caption="For home charging, use the electricity rate that applies when you charge and distinguish energy purchased from energy added to the battery." >}}

Suppose an EV has **60 kWh of usable battery capacity**, and you want to move from 20% to 80%.

The estimated energy added to the battery is:

**60 × (0.80 − 0.20) = 36 kWh.**

Energy drawn from the supply can be higher because charging is not perfectly efficient. If we assume **90% charging efficiency solely for this example**, the supply energy would be:

**36 ÷ 0.90 = 40 kWh.**

At an assumed **$0.20/kWh**, the energy cost would be **$8**.

The example is not a recommendation to use those charge limits for every vehicle. Follow your owner's manual. Actual usable capacity, temperature, charging losses and auxiliary energy use vary.

Also avoid adding the same losses twice. If the energy-consumption figure you are using already measures electricity purchased at the wall, do not automatically apply another charging-loss multiplier.

## Find the right number on your electricity bill

Look for the rate that applies when you will charge, not merely the largest number on the bill.

A time-of-use plan can have different peak and off-peak prices. Some charges vary with each kWh, while fixed monthly charges may exist regardless of whether you own an EV.

For the additional cost of charging, identify the charges that increase when you use more energy. For a full household budget, include fixed costs too—but do not attribute the entire existing electricity service charge to the car.

If an EV-specific tariff requires switching the whole home to different rates, compare the whole household bill before choosing it. Cheaper overnight charging can be offset by more expensive electricity at other times.

## Public charging: compare the final session price

{{< editorial-image name="ev-public-charging" alt="Illustration of electric crossovers parked at public charging bays" caption="Compare the full public-charging bill: energy, any time-based charge, membership costs and additional parking or idle fees." >}}

Check the rate shown in the station's app or on the charger before starting. Ask whether the price changes with membership, time, location or charging speed.

Per-minute billing requires special care: a slower session can change the cost even if you buy the same energy. A subscription can reduce the unit rate but still be poor value if you use that network infrequently.

For example, an assumed $10 monthly membership that saves $0.10 per kWh reaches its simple energy-price break-even at **100 kWh per month**. Below that, the subscription costs more on those assumptions; above it, it saves money before other fees. This is hypothetical arithmetic, not a quote from a charging provider.

Keep parking and idle fees separate. They can turn an apparently cheap energy price into an expensive stop.

## Build a realistic mixed-charging budget

Most owners should not assume every mile will be powered at their cheapest available rate.

Using the earlier 300 kWh monthly example, suppose 80% is bought at an assumed $0.15/kWh and 20% at $0.50/kWh:

- Home portion: 240 kWh × $0.15 = $36.
- Public portion: 60 kWh × $0.50 = $30.
- Total energy cost: **$66**, before other charges.

Notice that a fifth of the energy creates nearly half the energy bill in this example. That is why access to reliable charging can matter as much as a vehicle's headline efficiency.

For your own forecast, use actual mileage and a reasonable allowance for road trips, seasonal conditions and charging away from home. Recheck the estimate against real receipts after the first few months.

## Charging is one part of ownership cost

A lower energy bill does not establish that one vehicle is cheaper overall. Consider purchase or finance costs, insurance, tires, maintenance, depreciation and any charger installation.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is charging at home always cheaper?

No. Compare the actual home tariff with available public prices and fees. Free or discounted charging may exist, but availability and conditions can change.

### Should I multiply the whole battery size by the electricity rate?

Only for a suitable full-charge estimate, with clear capacity and loss assumptions. Most sessions add only part of the usable capacity.

### Why does my bill differ from my estimate?

Possible reasons include a different tariff, energy losses, weather, driving conditions, auxiliary consumption and extra fees. Compare billed kWh and prices before blaming the battery.

### Does a larger battery always cost more per mile?

No. Capacity tells you about stored energy, not by itself how efficiently the vehicle uses it. Compare energy use over distance.

## Use your rate, not somebody else's headline

Write down three things: your expected miles, a defensible energy-use figure and the prices where you will actually charge. That turns “Are EVs cheap to run?” into a budget you can check.
