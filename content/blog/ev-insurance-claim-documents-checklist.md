An insurance claim becomes harder to follow when the policy is in one inbox, the towing receipt is on paper and the repairer's diagnosis exists only in a phone conversation. You do not need a perfect filing system. You need records that show what happened, what was found and what each person agreed to do.

This guide sets out a practical document checklist for U.S. personal auto claims involving electric vehicles. The organisation suggestions are editorial guidance, not a universal insurer submission list; state rules, policy wording and the type of loss determine what is required.

**Short answer:** Keep your policy documents, incident details, safe photographs, inspection reports, estimates, authorisations, receipts and correspondence together. Add the battery warranty and finance paperwork where relevant, but do not assume that having a document proves coverage or that every claim needs every item. Safety comes before collecting evidence. [1][2][3]

## Start a claim folder before anything happens

Create one folder for the vehicle and one dated subfolder for each incident. A clear name such as “vehicle—date—claim” is more useful than an assortment of files called “insurance latest.” Keep the original documents alongside any working notes.

Save the policy schedule or declarations page, full policy wording and endorsements. An endorsement is a document that changes the policy; the summary alone may not tell you every condition. California's guide explains that a policy contains costs, coverages and exclusions, while its declarations identify key details. [2]

Also record the insurer's claim contact and the vehicle's identifying details. The NAIC advises reporting a claim promptly using the insurer's contact information and keeping notes of conversations with the adjuster, the person assessing the loss. [1]

This folder is a reference, not a reason to delay reporting. Tell the insurer what you have and ask how to provide anything missing.

## The EV claim document checklist

Use the table as an organising aid. Ask your adjuster which records apply to your particular incident before paying for additional reports.

| Record | What it helps explain | Important boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Policy, declarations and endorsements | Vehicle, dates, selected coverage and conditions | A summary is not the whole contract |
| Incident notes and safe photographs | What you observed and when | Do not approach a dangerous vehicle |
| Police or incident reference, if applicable | Where an official report can be obtained | Do not invent a report requirement |
| Inspection and diagnostic findings | What damage the repairer identified | A warning light is not a complete diagnosis |
| Written estimates and approvals | Proposed work and who authorised it | Approval to inspect is not necessarily repair approval |
| Towing, storage and transport receipts | Services provided and amounts charged | A receipt does not establish reimbursement |
| Warranty and ownership documents | Which separate agreement may be relevant | Warranty and insurance are different decisions |
| Valuation and finance records | Vehicle value and outstanding debt | The loan balance is not the vehicle's value |

The checklist draws on the NAIC's claim guidance, the FTC's repair-record advice and the state guidance cited below. It is not a prescribed national claim form. [1][2][3][4]

## Collect incident details without putting yourself at risk

The NAIC recommends recording the date, location and circumstances, exchanging the relevant information after a crash, identifying witnesses and asking how to obtain the police report when an officer attends. Photographs or a diagram can help explain the scene. [1]

For an EV, a normal-looking body panel does not establish that damaged high-voltage equipment is safe. NHTSA advises treating the high-voltage battery and associated components as energised after damage and not attempting repairs yourself. [5]

Record only what you can observe safely. Write “water visible inside the cabin” rather than “battery destroyed,” unless a qualified inspection has established the latter. Separate your observations, someone else's account and the repairer's findings.

If flooding is involved, follow the specific safety guidance in our [flood-damaged electric car insurance guide](/blog/does-car-insurance-cover-flood-damaged-electric-car/). Do not charge or move the vehicle simply to obtain better evidence.

## Keep the first report and every important update

Write down the claim reference, the name of the person handling it and the insurer's preferred submission method. In your own timeline, note when you reported the incident and when documents were requested or supplied.

The NAIC recommends keeping track of conversations and asking the adjuster for written explanations of decisions. A short follow-up message can identify what you understood and give the other person an opportunity to correct it. [1]

Keep a copy of what you sent, not just proof that an email was delivered. If you upload files through a portal, keep the original files and any confirmation available to you.

Ask the insurer to explain an unfamiliar request rather than guessing. “Please identify the document and why it is needed” is clearer than repeatedly sending an unrelated invoice.

## Ask for the diagnosis, not just the repair total

A repair estimate answers what work is proposed and what it may cost. The diagnosis should explain the condition being addressed. Keep both, especially where a battery or another high-voltage component is involved.

The FTC advises obtaining a written estimate identifying the condition, parts and anticipated labour, and clarifying how additional work will be authorised. It also notes that diagnostic work can carry a charge. [3]

Ask the repairer to distinguish confirmed findings from work that may become necessary after further inspection. If an estimate changes, retain the earlier version and the explanation rather than overwriting the history.

For a technical explanation of the work, use our [EV collision repair guide](/blog/electric-vehicle-collision-repair/). This document checklist does not prescribe a battery test or establish which repair procedure is appropriate.

## Separate inspection permission from repair approval

Before authorising substantial work, clarify the scope of the permission being requested. Ask whether you are approving diagnosis, dismantling, storage, repair or a combination, and who is agreeing to pay.

The FTC's U.S. repair guidance recommends a written estimate and an agreed process for work exceeding an authorised amount. Its explanation also makes clear that state requirements can matter. Do not treat a general conversation as a substitute for the written agreement. [3]

Keep the insurer's approval separately from your agreement with the repairer. They address related but different questions: what the shop may do and what the insurer considers payable under the policy.

If authorisation is unclear, ask for clarification in writing. This does not mean you should delay emergency action; follow responders' safety instructions and document arrangements when it is safe.

## Save receipts, but check the payment limits

Keep itemised towing, storage and alternative-transport records where those services are used. Record dates and providers so the insurer can identify the expense, rather than receiving an unexplained total.

Washington's regulator advises keeping roadside-service receipts and warns that rental-car limits can be exhausted before vehicle repairs finish. That is a reason to ask about authorisation, limits and end dates—not proof that every owner has rental or storage cover. [4]

Ask who is booking the service and whether payment is direct or reimbursement after you pay. A card charge shows money was spent; the policy and insurer's decision determine whether it is reimbursable.

For ongoing delays, our [EV repair delays and temporary transport guide](/blog/ev-insurance-repair-delays-rental-storage/) explains the questions to ask without assuming unlimited replacement transport.

## Keep warranty and finance records in separate groups

Include the warranty supplied with the vehicle, any relevant service record and the manufacturer's written decision if warranty coverage is considered. For example, Ford's U.S. 2026 guide distinguishes defect coverage from excluded damage causes; an insurance incident does not automatically become a warranty claim. [6]

For a financed vehicle, retain the finance agreement, recent balance information and any GAP agreement. The CFPB describes GAP as a product intended to address an eligible difference between the auto loan and insurance payment after theft or total loss, with restrictions that need checking. [7]

Do not label a loan statement “vehicle value.” Do not label a warranty booklet “battery insurance.” Accurate names help avoid confusion when documents move between a lender, insurer and repairer.

Our [battery warranty versus insurance guide](/blog/ev-battery-warranty-vs-insurance/) explains the distinction in more detail.

## Organise a disagreement around the disputed point

If the insurer declines an expense or proposes a settlement you question, ask for the reason and the relevant policy wording. Identify whether the disagreement concerns coverage, damage, repair scope, value or a charge.

The NAIC recommends written explanations and contacting the state insurance department if the disagreement cannot be resolved. It also suggests checking whether an appraisal clause applies to a valuation disagreement; that is not the same as a universal appeal right for every dispute. [1]

Prepare a short chronology, the decision, and the few documents that directly address it. A clearly identified estimate or photograph is easier to assess than an unlabelled archive of every conversation.

Use the NAIC's state-department directory to find the appropriate regulator. Do not assume another state's deadline or complaint procedure applies to your claim. [8]

## Share records carefully and keep the originals

Claim records can contain addresses, vehicle identifiers, finance details and other personal information. Use the insurer's confirmed contact route and ask which pages are actually needed before sharing a complete bundle.

As an editorial recordkeeping practice, keep originals and label any cropped photograph or annotated copy. If you correct an error in your notes, preserve enough context to show what changed.

Ask each organisation about its document requirements and retention process. This guide does not set a universal period for keeping claim files, and it does not replace advice about a legal dispute or a specific retention obligation.

Once the claim concludes, keep the final decision, invoices and correspondence together. A summary page can point to them, but it should not replace the supporting records.

## Frequently asked questions

**Must I collect everything before notifying the insurer?**

No. The NAIC advises prompt reporting. Report what happened, identify what is available and ask how to supply the rest. Do not wait for a complete folder while a policy reporting requirement goes unchecked. [1]

**Do I always need a police report?**

The requirement depends on the incident, local rules and policy. The NAIC explains what report information to collect when an officer attends and advises reporting theft or a hit-and-run to police. Ask what applies to your circumstances. [1]

**Should I open the battery enclosure for photographs?**

No. NHTSA advises against attempting repairs to damaged electric vehicles and warns about exposed high-voltage components. Leave access and inspection to appropriately qualified professionals. [5]

**Does a repair estimate guarantee payment?**

No. An estimate describes proposed work and cost; coverage and authorisation remain separate questions. Obtain the repair agreement and insurer's decision rather than treating either as interchangeable. [2][3]

**Will all rental receipts be reimbursed?**

Not necessarily. Washington's regulator warns that rental limits may run out before repairs finish. Confirm your actual coverage, authorisation and limits before relying on reimbursement. [4]

**Does my battery warranty replace the insurance policy?**

No. A warranty has its own terms and excluded causes. Keep the actual booklet and any warranty decision, alongside rather than in place of the auto policy. The Ford example is manufacturer-specific. [6]

**Who can help with an unresolved claim-handling problem?**

The NAIC recommends your state insurance department. Use its directory to locate the regulator and check that office's process; retain the insurer's decision and the supporting records. [1][8]
