If you buy an electric truck, van or SUV for your business in the United States, federal tax depreciation rules can change what the vehicle really costs you. One of the best-known is Section 179 of the Internal Revenue Code, which lets a business elect to deduct the cost of qualifying property in the year it is placed in service, instead of depreciating it over several years.

But the rules for vehicles are more complicated than "buy an EV and write it off." What you can deduct depends on the vehicle's weight rating, its body type, how much you use it for business, your taxable business income, and how Section 179 interacts with bonus depreciation. This guide explains the 2026 limits and the vehicle rules in plain language, using IRS sources, so you know what to ask your tax professional.

**Short answer:** For tax years beginning in 2026, the maximum Section 179 deduction is $2,560,000, and it phases out above $4,090,000 of qualifying purchases. A heavy SUV (rated over 6,000 and up to 14,000 pounds) is capped at $32,000 under Section 179, although some pickups and vans are exempt from that cap. You generally need more than 50% business use, and 100% bonus depreciation can cover more of the cost. This is general information, not tax advice.

## What is Section 179?

Section 179 allows a business to treat the cost of qualifying property as an expense in the year it is placed in service, up to a dollar limit, rather than recovering the cost gradually through regular depreciation.

It is not an EV-specific incentive. It is a general business-expensing rule that applies to eligible equipment and certain vehicles, so a qualifying gasoline work truck and a qualifying electric work truck are treated under the same rules. What makes EVs interesting is simply that a business EV is depreciable business property like any other vehicle.

## The 2026 Section 179 limits

IRS Publication 946 lists these limits for tax years beginning in 2026 ([IRS Pub. 946](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p946.pdf)):

| Item | 2026 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Maximum Section 179 deduction | $2,560,000 | $2,500,000 |
| Reduced once qualifying property placed in service exceeds | $4,090,000 | $4,000,000 |
| Maximum for sport utility vehicles | $32,000 | $31,300 |

The $2,560,000 figure applies to all of your qualifying property for the year, not to each vehicle. The deduction is reduced dollar for dollar by the amount your qualifying purchases exceed $4,090,000. Section 179 is also limited by your taxable business income, so it cannot create a loss on its own; check Publication 946 or your tax professional about carryforwards.

## Which electric work vehicles may qualify?

There is no list of "Section 179 EVs." What matters is the vehicle's gross vehicle weight rating (GVWR), its body type and its use. Three categories matter most.

| Vehicle category | Section 179 and depreciation treatment |
|---|---|
| Passenger automobile rated 6,000 lb or less | Total depreciation is capped each year under Section 280F (2026 first-year cap: $20,300 with bonus depreciation) |
| "SUV" rated over 6,000 and up to 14,000 lb | Section 179 capped at $32,000; bonus depreciation may cover the rest |
| Heavier vehicle that meets an exception (see below) | Not subject to the $32,000 SUV cap |

Because many electric cars and crossovers weigh in near the 6,000-pound line, check the exact GVWR on the vehicle's door-jamb label or the manufacturer's specifications. Do not rely on curb weight or marketing descriptions.

### Passenger automobiles rated 6,000 pounds or less

This category matters for many EV buyers. A four-wheeled vehicle rated at 6,000 pounds or less is a "passenger automobile," and its total yearly depreciation, including Section 179 and bonus depreciation, is capped by Section 280F. For vehicles placed in service in 2026, the IRS limits in Revenue Procedure 2026-15 are $20,300 in the first year when bonus depreciation applies ($12,300 if it does not), then $19,800, $11,900 and $7,160 a year after that ([Tax Facts via ThinkAdvisor](https://www.thinkadvisor.com/tax-facts/2026/03/12/irs-releases-2026-limits-for-passenger-automobile-depreciation/), [Current Federal Tax Developments](https://www.currentfederaltaxdevelopments.com/blog/2026/3/3/analysis-of-revenue-procedure-2026-15-passenger-automobile-depreciation-limitations-and-lease-inclusion-amounts-for-2026)).

### Heavy SUVs: the $32,000 cap

Publication 946 says the SUV limit applies to a four-wheeled vehicle primarily designed or used to carry passengers on public roads that is rated at more than 6,000 pounds and not more than 14,000 pounds GVWR. For 2026, the cap is $32,000.

### Pickups and vans: the exceptions

Under Publication 946, the SUV limit does not apply to a vehicle that is:

- designed to seat more than nine passengers behind the driver's seat;
- equipped with a cargo area, open or enclosed by a cap, of at least 6 feet in interior length that is not readily accessible from the passenger compartment; or
- built with an integral enclosure fully enclosing the driver compartment and load-carrying area, with no seating behind the driver's seat and no body section protruding more than 30 inches ahead of the windshield.

(The IRS publication states these exceptions in its discussion of the SUV limit; confirm the current wording in the latest edition.)

For electric work vehicles this means:

- **Electric pickups:** measure the interior bed length. If it is under six feet, the pickup may be treated like an SUV for this rule. Bed length varies by model and cab, so check yours.
- **Electric cargo vans:** a van with a fully enclosed cargo area and no seating behind the driver may fall under the third exception. Whether yours does depends on its exact configuration, so ask your tax professional to confirm.
- **Vehicles over 14,000 pounds** are outside the SUV definition.

Even when the SUV cap does not apply, the other requirements still do: business use, your income limit and the overall Section 179 limit.

## The business-use test: more than 50%

Publication 946 says property that is not used predominantly, meaning more than 50%, for qualified business use cannot support a Section 179 deduction or a special depreciation allowance. Vehicles are "listed property," so you need records.

If you use a vehicle 80% for business, only the business share of its cost is depreciable. On an $80,000 EV, that is $64,000 before any caps. At 40% business use, Section 179 is not available.

Keep a mileage log with the date, destination, business purpose, starting and ending mileage, total annual mileage and personal mileage. These records are how you show the percentage if the IRS asks.

## Section 179 vs. bonus depreciation

Bonus depreciation, under Section 168(k), is a separate first-year deduction. The 2025 tax law restored 100% bonus depreciation for qualifying property acquired after January 19, 2025, and tax guides describe it as covering vehicles that meet the rules. Section 179 is generally applied first, and bonus depreciation can then apply to the remaining basis ([CatchingNumbers](https://catchingnumbers.com/blog/bonus-depreciation-on-vehicles-2025-and-2026-deduction-guide/)).

| | Section 179 | Bonus depreciation |
|---|---|---|
| Law | IRC Section 179 | IRC Section 168(k) |
| 2026 amount | Up to $2,560,000, with phase-out | 100% for qualifying property acquired after January 19, 2025 |
| Heavy SUV | $32,000 cap | Separate rules apply |
| Election | You elect it | Generally applies unless you elect out |
| Passenger car (6,000 lb or less) | Counts toward the yearly 280F cap | Counts toward the yearly 280F cap |

Because the two provisions interact, and your business structure and state rules matter, a tax professional should run the numbers for your situation. Some states do not follow federal bonus depreciation or Section 179 limits, so check your state.

## An illustration (not a quote)

Assume an $80,000 electric SUV rated above 6,000 pounds, placed in service in 2026, with qualifying business use and no other limits in play:

| Business use | Business basis | Section 179 (capped) | Remaining basis that bonus depreciation may cover |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100% | $80,000 | $32,000 | $48,000 |
| 80% | $64,000 | $32,000 | $32,000 |
| 40% | Does not qualify | None | See your tax professional |

This is arithmetic from the caps above, not a promise of any deduction. Your income limit, tax elections and the vehicle's classification can change the result.

## Used EVs and leases

A used electric vehicle can qualify if it meets the rules, including that the property is acquired by purchase and not, for example, from certain related parties. Check Publication 946.

If you lease the vehicle, you do not own it, so Section 179 on the purchase price generally does not apply to you. A lessee generally looks at lease payments and the business-use rules, and for passenger autos, Revenue Procedure 2026-15 also publishes lease inclusion amounts. The specific lease terms matter.

## What if business use falls to 50% or less?

Recapture is a trap. IRS guidance says you may have to recapture part of a Section 179 deduction if, in any year of the property's recovery period, business use drops to 50% or less, even though you have not sold the vehicle ([IRS Pub. 946](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p946.pdf)). The recapture is figured on Form 4797. Keep logging mileage every year, not just the first.

## Section 179 is not the EV tax credit

They are different things. Section 179 is a depreciation deduction. The clean vehicle credits are tax credits, and the IRS says the 2025 law accelerated the termination of the clean vehicle credits under sections 30D, 25E and 45W, which covered new, previously owned and commercial clean vehicles ([IRS bulletin](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USIRS/bulletins/3f4df63)). For vehicles acquired after September 30, 2025, you should not assume you can claim them; see IRS Fact Sheet 2025-05 for the details. That does not by itself rule out depreciation deductions.

## How to claim Section 179 for an EV

1. **Confirm eligibility.** Get the GVWR, seating layout, cargo-area dimensions and purchase and in-service dates.
2. **Establish business use.** Calculate your business-use percentage from a mileage log.
3. **Check the limits.** Apply the 2026 Section 179 limits, the SUV cap or the passenger-car caps, and your taxable-income limit.
4. **Consider bonus depreciation.** Decide whether it applies, or whether electing out makes sense.
5. **Report it.** Businesses generally use IRS Form 4562 for Section 179 and depreciation. Requirements vary by business type.
6. **Keep records.** Keep the purchase agreement, proof of payment, title, GVWR documentation, mileage logs and depreciation calculations.

## Common mistakes

- Assuming a heavy SUV can be fully expensed under Section 179 alone. The cap is $32,000.
- Assuming every pickup or van escapes the cap. Check the bed length and configuration.
- Overlooking the 6,000-pound line and the passenger-car caps.
- Skipping the mileage log.
- Letting business use slip to 50% or less in a later year.
- Treating Section 179 and the old EV tax credits as the same thing.
- Using a vehicle's advertised weight instead of its GVWR.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the Section 179 limit for 2026?**
For tax years beginning in 2026, the maximum is $2,560,000, reduced once qualifying property placed in service exceeds $4,090,000, according to IRS Publication 946.

**Can I deduct an entire electric SUV under Section 179?**
Not under Section 179 alone if it is a heavy SUV; the 2026 cap is $32,000. Bonus depreciation may cover more of the cost, depending on the rules.

**Do electric pickups face the $32,000 cap?**
Not if the pickup has a cargo area at least 6 feet long inside that is not readily accessible from the passenger compartment. Check your model and bed length.

**Is there a business-use requirement?**
Yes. Generally, the vehicle must be used more than 50% for qualified business use.

**What happens if my business use drops later?**
If business use falls to 50% or less during the recovery period, you may have to recapture part of the deduction.

**Can I use Section 179 on a leased EV?**
Generally no, because Section 179 applies to property you purchase. Lease payments follow different rules.

**Is there still a federal EV tax credit?**
The IRS says the 2025 law terminated the clean vehicle credits under sections 30D, 25E and 45W, so you should not assume a credit for vehicles acquired after September 30, 2025.

**Should I use a tax professional?**
Yes. Vehicle classification, bonus depreciation, state rules and your income limit all affect the outcome.
