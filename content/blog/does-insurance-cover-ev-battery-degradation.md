Every EV battery loses some capacity over time — that's normal chemistry, not a malfunction. [As covered in our guide on what's actually covered](/blog/is-ev-battery-replacement-covered-by-insurance/), auto insurance treats this the same way it treats worn brake pads: an ownership cost, not an insurable loss. So if insurance doesn't cover degradation, what does?

**Short answer: your manufacturer's battery warranty does — but only once capacity loss crosses a specific threshold, commonly around 70% of the battery's original capacity.** Below that line, it's just normal aging. Cross it, and it becomes a warranty claim.

### The 70% rule, by automaker

| Automaker | Battery warranty | Capacity guarantee |
|---|---|---|
| Tesla | 8 years (mileage varies by model, 100k–150k mi) | At least 70% |
| GM / Chevrolet / Cadillac | 8 years / 100,000 miles | 70% (check exact model year — some documents use 75%) |
| Rivian | 8 years / 175,000 miles | 70% |
| Hyundai / Kia / Genesis | Up to 10 years / 100,000 miles | 70% |

*Always confirm your specific vehicle's terms — thresholds and mileage limits vary by brand and model year.*

### Where the line actually gets drawn

Think of it as two separate systems handling two separate problems:

- **Warranty territory:** Gradual capacity loss that eventually drops *below* the guaranteed threshold (usually 70%) during the warranty period. This is an internal battery-health issue.
- **Insurance territory:** Sudden capacity loss or destruction caused by something *external* — a crash, flood, fire, or road debris puncturing the pack. This is what [we broke down by real dollar cost in our replacement pricing guide](/blog/ev-battery-replacement-cost-2026/).

The confusion usually happens because both can eventually lead to the same outcome — "my range dropped" or "my battery needs replacing" — but insurers and warranty administrators are asking a completely different question to decide who pays: *was this caused by wear, or by an event?*

### What if your battery degrades faster than normal — but stays above the threshold?

This is the real gap most owners don't know exists. If your capacity drops noticeably but technically stays above the warranty's guaranteed floor, you're in a genuine coverage no-man's-land: not bad enough for a warranty claim, not caused by an insurable event either. Some owners in this position look at extended battery protection plans as a middle option — worth weighing on their own terms, which we cover separately.

### Frequently asked questions

**How is battery capacity actually measured?**
Typically via a diagnostic test at a dealer or specialist (state-of-health, or SOH, reported as a percentage of original capacity) — not something you can reliably self-diagnose from range alone.

**Does fast charging accelerate degradation faster than the warranty accounts for?**
Charging habits can influence degradation rate, but the warranty threshold itself doesn't change based on how you charged — it's a flat capacity-percentage guarantee regardless of usage pattern.

**If my battery is damaged in an accident, does that count toward the degradation threshold?**
No — accident damage is an insurance claim, evaluated on its own, separate from any gradual capacity-loss tracking.
